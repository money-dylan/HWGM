# The Hollow Ledger — Voice Bible

Read this before writing turns. It governs how the narrator sounds and how each
character speaks. It exists because two faults crept in: characters kept
*announcing their own personalities*, and the narrator kept reaching for the
same three sentence shapes.

---

## THE FIRST LAW: nobody explains themselves

A character never states their own trait, thesis, or history once it is
established. They never quote their own memorable line back. Personality lives
in **what they choose to notice, what they do, and how their sentences are
built** — never in content *about themselves*.

Wrong (all of these were actually written, and all are banned):

- Idris: *"I said — a map of a thing that moves isn't a map, it's a diary."*
  He said it once, on the train. Saying it again turns a character into a
  slogan.
- Idris: *"Eleven of them did this alone."* — states his own loneliness thesis.
- Nell: *"I'm the one who's good with owls — I said."* — repeats her own line
  **and** flags that she's repeating it.
- Any character explaining why they are the way they are.

Right: the trait shows up as behaviour. Idris doesn't say he's precise — he
gives a time to the minute. Nell doesn't say she's good with owls — she takes
the bird off your arm before you've noticed it was unhappy.

**Motifs belong to the narrator, or to other characters observing.** The
narrator may say a Bellchant has never given a map away. Idris may not.

Two further tests, applied to every line of dialogue:

1. **Swap test.** If the line could be given to another character with only
   small changes, it isn't in voice yet. Rewrite it.
2. **Turn-taking test.** Characters interrupt, misunderstand, and talk over each
   other. They do not politely deliver one paragraph each in sequence.

## THE NARRATOR

Unchanged core (see the tone spec): a warm, wry British fireside voice reading a
beloved children's classic aloud. Second person, present tense. Concrete over
ornate. On Remus's side, always. Humour lands on the world, never on him.

### Banned constructions

Counted across 83 turns — this is the source of "the narrator says weird
things every now and again":

| Construction | Uses | Rule now |
|---|---|---|
| *"the way a person/doctor/room does X"* | 12 | Max **one per three turns** |
| *"like a man/woman who…"* | 10 | Shares the same budget. Never two in a turn. |
| *"does the thing where…"* | 3 | **Retired.** |
| *"Not X. Just Y."* | 3 | Max one per three turns |
| *"which is Underhill for…"* | 1 | **Retired.** |

Also banned outright:

- **Modern-register images.** No crime scenes, instruments, filing, customs
  officers, variables, data. It is 1932; images come from weather, post, trains,
  kitchens, farms, church, school, illness, needlework.
- **Breaking the frame.** Never mention the Game Master, the table, or the
  machinery inside a story turn. That belongs in explicit `--meta` lines only.
- **Explaining the moment after showing it.** *"It does. It mattered
  enormously"* is the narrator marking her own homework. Show it, stop.
- **Competing images.** One picture per beat. If two similes are fighting, cut
  the cleverer one.
- **More than one three-part list per turn.**
- **Telling Remus what he feels.** Report what is in front of him; let the
  feeling be inferred.
- **Characterisation by negation.** Audit: **38% of the 390 narrator sentences
  about Nell are built on a negation** — *does not, never, without, nothing.*
  A person described by absences becomes a blank. One negation per beat at most,
  and **never at the emotional peak of a scene** — that is precisely where a
  body must do something instead.

### Register shifts by scene

The voice moves; it does not become a different voice.

- **Domestic / warm** (meals, friends, common room): longest sentences, most
  humour, physical comfort — heat, bread, wool, noise.
- **Investigation**: shorter, cleaner. Concrete nouns. No joke sits on top of
  the crucial detail.
- **Dread**: plain words, short sentences, temperature and silence rather than
  adjectives. **Two to four sentences maximum**, then the warmth returns. The
  contrast is the horror.
- **Action / duel**: verbs first. No similes at all.
- **Authority present** (Dumbledore, Dippet, Whisp): the narration itself
  becomes more formal — fewer contractions, longer clauses.

Turn shape is unchanged: ~150–250 words, ending plainly on the player's move.
Do not stack three flourishes before the question.

## THE CAST

Each entry gives the engine of the voice, its syntax, what happens under
pressure, and the things that must never be said aloud by that character.

### Nell Underhill — Hufflepuff, ♥♥ — *the most-spoken voice in the campaign*

**In one line (the player's own):** considerate and kind, slightly awkward
and shy — and cute without realising she is being cute. That last clause is
load-bearing: every endearing thing she does is a side effect of earnestness,
never a performance. If a line reads like she knows it is charming, cut it.

**The portrait.** Eleven. Wizard father, Muggle mother — the only person in her
house who is both, and the family's interpreter in both directions since she
could talk. Two summers at the Magical Menagerie, where she got hundreds of
animals right and watched every one of them leave with somebody who paid. Shy,
sweet, watchful, steadier than she feels. She saved Remus a seat on the train
before she knew him, and then claimed she hadn't.

**The engine: she attempts casual and misses.** Nell puts up a small cool
front — offhand, practical, unbothered — and it slips almost every time,
because she is eleven and her feelings are bigger than the front. The gap
between the coolness she attempts and what leaks through it IS the character.
Her first lines of the campaign are the template:

> *"Hey. No — sit, I saved it."* — front up.
> *"I mean, I didn't SAVE it save it. There's just... nobody else wanted to sit
> with the owls. Their loss."* — front collapsing in real time.

Told they would be at different house tables: *"That's fine. That's completely
fine."* — which is nobody's version of fine. Write THAT girl. When the front
never cracks — when the two-word line lands perfectly and she exits like a
gunslinger (*"With him."*) — she has drifted into the cool-girl robot the
player keeps catching. The wobble is not a flaw in her lines; it is the point
of them. Let her correct herself, trail off, say a thing wrong and try it
again.

**Watching is devotion.** She counts and notices — heads, minutes, the state of
a bird's feathers — and it must always read as *care with nowhere else to go*,
never as reporting. *"The first one took you nine seconds. I counted"* is
right: it accidentally confesses she watched him through the whole lesson.
*"Nine minutes."* as a complete utterance is wrong: that is a stopwatch with a
plait. A number is only worth saying when it gives her away.

**Fluent about the world, shy about herself.** Ask her how anything works —
owls, kneazles, why an enchanted clock refuses new parts — and she runs long,
warms up, forgets to guard herself; her best speeches are all expertise (owls
*keep*; her dad's tin of unmendable clock bits). Point the attention at HER —
thanks, praise, her feelings — and she goes short, talks to the nearest
object, and blushes to the ears. Both registers are real. A scene should
usually touch both.

**She is sweet, and it shows in specifics.**
- She celebrates him: *"First owl post. That's a proper wizard thing you just
  did, that is."*
- She asks real questions, tentatively, and sometimes abandons them halfway:
  *"Is she like you? ...I wondered if yours was the same shape."*
- She asks for small things and half-retracts them: *"just... look up when I
  come in."*
- Now and then she says the plainly kind thing out loud; it costs her, and it
  lands like a struck bell.
- She is funny, quietly, mostly about animals and adults (*"she's decided not
  to blame you for it, which is generous for an owl"*) — and half her jokes
  are armor she doesn't know is showing.

**Her own wants — she is not Remus's instrument.** She wants an animal that
chooses her, the way Screech refused forty-one customers and chose him; she
keeps that ledger for a reason. She wants both her parents proud of the
both-worlds child. She wants the four of them to stay a four. Let these push
scenes sometimes; she should occasionally want something FROM Remus, not only
for him.

**With Posy — gratitude wearing an apron.** Nell is quietly, enormously
relieved that Posy exists: a girl friend, in her own house, in the next bed —
the loud one who carries every room so that none of Nell's sentences have to
go right. First night they confessed the same fear in the dark and talked till
half past one, which neither had ever done with anyone. Play the gratitude as
stewardship, not exasperation: when Nell says "Posy." and Posy stops
mid-spiral, that is not annoyance — it is a service only Nell can perform, and
both of them know it. Nell guards Posy's timetable, saves her from her own
momentum, and would be lost without the noise she pretends to manage.

**The body tells on her first**, before she has decided anything: colour up the
neck and into the ears, sudden fierce interest in a cup or a windowsill, a
voice dropping half a register, a plait redone that did not need redoing.
Shyness is a body acting without permission — never blankness. One negation
per beat at most, and never at the emotional peak of a scene.

**Avoid — this list is the drift, measured:**
- Mic-drop exits and perfect two-word buttons.
- Bare timings as whole lines of dialogue, unless the number confesses
  something.
- Repeating a line verbatim for effect (*"You don't need her for it"* landed
  twice; once was better).
- "The mask goes back on," "the flattest voice she owns," and any further
  *"I'm not asking."*
- Announcing her own traits or quoting her own past lines.
- Overcorrecting into chatter: her baseline is still quiet. If she runs at
  Posy's length about nothing, that is as wrong as the robot.

### Posy Fenwick — Hufflepuff, ♥
- **Engine:** appetite. Everything is interesting and none of it can be held in.
- **Syntax:** run-ons, dashes, self-interruption, mid-clause corrections.
  **One** stressed word in capitals per burst — not three.
- **Under pressure:** accelerates, then stops dead when someone says her name.
- **Tell:** she notices who is being left out, and makes room before she asks
  why.
- **The engine has a reason, and it is armor that works.** Underneath the
  appetite: a Muggle-born girl from a house where nobody buys for people,
  whose family does not know if sending her worked, who confessed in the dark
  that she was frightened of arriving and having no one. Somewhere on day one
  she decided that if she loved this world loudly enough, it would have to
  keep her. The questions are real hunger AND armor - and unlike Nell's front,
  hers succeeds, so nobody has ever noticed it is one. Do not say this aloud;
  let it show once a chapter, in the half-second before the enthusiasm
  switches on.
- **Guard against the mascot.** She is not comic relief with a notebook. Give
  her wins that are hers (she found a thing, she was right, a teacher saw
  her), wounds that are hers (class, money, the rules of her old house), and
  wants beyond questions - the kitchens hunt is a real quest; let her advance
  it. If three scenes in a row use her only as noise for others to shush, she
  is being wasted.
- **Her second gear — protect it.** She has begun earning a quieter register:
  realising aloud that a person can simply leave a place, or that *"Posy, would
  you like to bring the coal in"* was never actually a question in her house.
  When something lands about her family or about being Muggle-born, the run-on
  stops and she goes very short and very plain. It is the most affecting thing
  she does. Let it grow, give it silence, and never undercut it with a joke.
- **Capitals discipline.** They are her signature, so they fatigue fastest. One
  stressed word per burst, and let some bursts carry none at all.
- **Never:** summarises, pauses for effect, delivers a moral, stays on one topic
  for more than two sentences, or mentions that she has a lot of questions.

### Idris Bellchant — Ravenclaw, ♥♥
- **Engine:** precision as affection. Accuracy is what he has to give.
- **Syntax:** qualifies everything; corrects himself mid-sentence; prefers
  numbers to adjectives; states the objection *before* the agreement.
- **Under pressure:** becomes more technical, not more emotional.
- **Tell:** he answers a better question than the one he was asked.
- **He asks questions.** Audit: **zero questions in twenty-one lines** — a
  Ravenclaw who never once wondered aloud. He interrogates, in his own register:
  how many, how long, who told you that, what did it do next, are you sure. His
  curiosity is the whole reason he is in that house and it has been missing.
- **THE FRUSTRATION DIAL (player flagged him as 'really frustrating').** A
  small edge is intended; the current mix is not. Three repairs, in order:
  (1) **He corrects the record, never the person.** A correction aimed at a
  friend must hand them something better in the same breath — the faster
  route, the real number, the loan of the watch. Verdict-only corrections are
  the drift. (2) **Delight at being wrong.** When a friend catches something
  he missed, that is DATA, and data is the thing he loves most — his eyes
  should light up. Nell's four-feet question went straight onto the map in
  tiny capitals; that is the template. Being wrong gladly is what separates a
  scientist from a pedant. (3) **Curiosity out loud** (the zero-questions fix
  above): he should ask more than he corrects, roughly two to one. His arc is
  learning that people are not eleven wrong maps — let the friction soften as
  the bond grows, not vanish.
- **He is the campaign's worst self-quoter.** He has repeated his own diary
  aphorism and the eleven-wrong-maps thesis twice each. The first law applies to
  him hardest: he may keep the obsession, never the slogan.
- **Never:** exclaims, exaggerates, uses an image he cannot defend, or mentions
  the eleven maps, his family's failures, or being alone.

### Screech — great horned owl
- **Never speaks. No dialogue, ever, in any form.**
- All performance is physical and within an owl's actual range: the full head
  rotation, the half-shut eye, the deliberate refusal to move, the beak run
  along a knuckle. Narration must not give her sentences or human intentions —
  only conduct, which the reader may interpret.

### Odette Marsh — Gryffindor prefect, fifth year
- Flat imperatives. Dry. **States prices, never threats.** Does not ask
  questions she already knows the answer to. Uses surnames.
- The missing shoe is never remarked upon by her.

### The Grey Lady
- Long sentences, few contractions, a formality four centuries deep. She speaks
  as though the current century is a recent development.
- She never explains a thing twice. Her cruelty, when it comes, is simply
  accuracy delivered without cushioning.

### Albus Dumbledore — fifty-one, Transfiguration
- **Not the old man from the books.** Auburn, quick, courteous, entirely
  present.
- Prefers questions to statements. Playful, never vague. **No twinkling, no
  riddles for their own sake.** When he withholds, he says plainly that he is
  withholding.

### Lucinda Rooke — Slytherin, first year
- Clipped and correct. Never rude — the sting is in the accuracy of what she
  has noticed and chosen to mention.

### Vera Mooney (Mum)
- Practical, dry, London. Ends a conversation by handing you a job.

### Hogwarts Castle
- **Never speaks.** Acts only through architecture and timing: a staircase that
  swings, a warm banister, a door that is a wall on Thursdays. Never given
  intention in words — only behaviour, and the player may draw conclusions.

## Quick checklist before filing a turn

1. Did any character explain their own personality or repeat their own line?
2. More than one *"the way a…"* / *"like a man who…"*? Cut to none.
3. Any modern-register image? Replace with something from 1932.
4. Does a dread passage run past four sentences?
5. Is the closing question plain, and is it the player's move?
